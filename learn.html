How Mid/Side works

Why you can't split the S file into "left" and "right", and how a single subtraction still gets you left and right.

The one idea to take away A recording channel is not a sound. It is a mix of every sound around the microphone, each one turned up or down depending on where it comes from. Channels always overlap. Stereo is made by combining channels with simple arithmetic, not by finding a channel that only contains the left side.

1Every microphone hears everything

A microphone capsule is a sensor that responds to sound from all directions, just more strongly from some than from others. The shape of that sensitivity is its polar pattern. The diagrams below show the microphone from above, facing the top of the page. The further the line reaches in a direction, the louder sound from there is recorded.

A Zoom recorder in 4-channel mode is a small sensor array: two capsules for XY and two for Mid/Side, each with its own pattern, all listening to the same room at the same time. Every capsule records the whole scene. They differ only in how much of each direction they let in, and the S capsule also differs in which way up (polarity) the signal comes out.

2The cappuccino: why this feels impossible

Pour milk into coffee and you get a cappuccino. Try to get the milk back out. You can't. Once they're mixed, they're mixed. We've learned this in kitchens all our lives, so when we hear that the left and right sounds were "mixed" into a recording, our intuition says they're gone for good.

That intuition comes from the physical world, and signals don't live there. A digital audio signal is just a long list of numbers, one per sample. Mixing two signals means adding those numbers, and any addition can be undone with a subtraction. Numbers can also go below zero, which milk can't: "minus one cup of milk" means nothing in a kitchen, but a signal multiplied by −1 (polarity inverted) is perfectly real. Call it anti-milk.

The signal version of a cappuccino

With signals, coffee = M (what both ears share) and milk = S (the left–right difference). Making stereo is mixing. Getting M and S back is unmixing. Both are one operation:

mix: L = M + S unmix: M = (L + R) / 2 R = M − S S = (L − R) / 2

Try it with one sample. Say coffee M = 0.3 and milk S = 0.1. Mixing gives L = 0.4 and R = 0.2. Unmixing gives (0.4 + 0.2) / 2 = 0.3 and (0.4 − 0.2) / 2 = 0.1: the exact coffee and milk you started with, with nothing lost. Do that for every sample, 48,000 times a second, and you've decoded a recording.

The one rule the kitchen still teaches us
To undo a mix you need as many different mixes as there are ingredients. M and S are two different cups, so L and R can be computed. The S file on its own is a single cup with "left" and "right" already inside it, so no subtraction can split it. That's why S can't be separated into two: S was never two signals.

We can always compute the mix because the microphone is the recipe: the polar patterns fix exactly how every direction is added into M and S. Separating actual sources, like a guitar from a voice, is harder, because there nobody hands you the recipe (see going further).

4How much of each sound ends up where

Picture a small band in front of the recorder: a guitar 60° to the left, a voice straight ahead and a drum 60° to the right. The table shows how much of each one is in each channel, with 1.00 meaning the full sound. This is the "overlap" made visible. The table follows the width slider in the playground.

  • No row is "pure". Even the decoded L channel holds the whole voice and some of the drum. L doesn't mean "only left". It means "left is louder here than in R".
  • S has the voice at 0.00. The front is S's blind spot, so anything in the centre lives only in M.
  • S has the guitar and the drum with opposite signs. This is the one cup with both inside, which is why S can't be split.
  • XY overlaps too. The XY left capsule still hears the drum on the right. Real arrays always overlap. Stereo comes from the difference in level between the two channels.

5Two S files, one signal

The converter gives you _S_plus.wav and _S_minus.wav. They are not two halves of S. They are the same signal, one upside-down:

A sound from the left reaches the figure-8's + lobe, so it comes out of S the same way up as in M. When you add them (L = M + S), the two waves reinforce each other. A sound from the right reaches the − lobe and comes out upside-down, so in L it partly cancels M, while in R (= M − S, i.e. M plus the flipped S) it reinforces. Put S+ on the left speaker and S− on the right, add M to both, and you have built the decoder yourself.

6Where XY fits in (it doesn't enter the equation)

In 4-channel mode the recorder runs two separate arrays at once and saves them as two separate files. They are two independent ways of recording the same scene, not four ingredients of one recipe.

XY.WAV: already stereo

channel 1 = L (aims left) channel 2 = R (aims right)

The two XY capsules are physically pointed left and right, so each one is a left or right microphone. There's nothing to decode. In the converter, "Split channels" just hands you L and R as they were recorded.

MS.WAV: stereo after one step

channel 1 = M, channel 2 = S L = M + S R = M − S

Here no capsule points left or right, so L and R have to be built. Every term in the equation is M or S. XY isn't there.

Why XY can't be used in the MS equation

  • The equation is already complete. Two unknowns (L and R), two cups with known recipes (M and S). In cappuccino terms: you already have both cups you need to get the coffee and the milk back. A third and fourth cup can't make the answer "more correct".
  • XY cups come from a different kitchen. XY's recipes are cardioids at ±45°, not M's and S's. Mixing them into L = M + S changes the recipe into something that is neither MS nor XY, and the virtual microphones stop pointing where you think.
  • The capsules sit in different places. The XY pair is a few centimetres away from the MS pair, so sound reaches them a tiny bit earlier or later. Adding signals with different arrival times cancels some frequencies and boosts others (comb filtering), and it sounds hollow and "phasey". M and S work together only because they are mounted at the same point.

So you end up with two stereo versions of the same take: XY (fixed angle, ready to use) and decoded MS (adjustable width, centre-safe in mono). Tick Compare with XY in the playground to see how similar they are. You pick one, or blend the two finished stereo files later in a DAW as a creative choice, by ear. Check the image isn't mirrored: on some models the two arrays face different directions.

You don't need Audacity

The converter already does the whole decode. Decoded stereo (_LR.wav) is a finished stereo file with L in the left channel and R in the right. You can play it, hear it with the converter's own ▶ Preview, or drop it straight into any editor or project.

We use Audacity in class only as an external graphical tool: a second, independent way to look at the waveforms and to build the decoder by hand, so the maths isn't a black box.

Optional: build the decoder yourself in Audacity

  1. Download M, S + and S − and import all three into Audacity as mono tracks.
  2. Pan M to the centre, S+ fully left and S− fully right.
  3. Play. You're hearing L = M + S and R = M − S, made by the mixer.
  4. Turn both S tracks down by the same amount: the image gets narrower. Turn them up: wider. Mute them: mono. That's the width slider.
  5. Swap the pans of S+ and S−: left and right swap. That's the "Invert S" checkbox.

8Common mix-ups

"S contains the left and the right sounds, so I can split it into two files."

S contains both mixed into one signal (left the right way up, right upside-down). One cup, two ingredients: there is no operation that unmixes it on its own. You need M as the second cup.

"The L channel only contains what was on the left."

L contains everything, with the left side louder. Look at the table: the voice in the middle is fully in both L and R. That's exactly why it sounds centred.

"I need the XY file to decode the MS file" (or "L + R uses all four channels").

Decoding MS uses only M and S. The XY file is a second, independent stereo recording that is already L and R. Adding its channels to the MS equation doesn't help and can make the sound phasey, because the capsules are in different places. See where XY fits in.

"S− is the right-hand half of S."

S− is the whole of S, flipped upside-down. Its only job is to be subtracted from M for the right channel.

"More microphones means the sounds come out separated."

More microphones give you more cups with different recipes, so you can steer virtual microphones towards a direction. Every channel is still a mix. Pulling individual instruments out of a recording ("stem separation") is a different, statistical problem.

"Inverting the polarity is the same as a time delay."

No. Inverting multiplies every sample by −1, so every frequency flips at once with no delay. For a single steady sine wave the two can look alike on screen, but on real sound they are different operations.

9Try this in the playground

Put the sound straight in front. Why are L and R exactly equal?

The front is in the gap between the figure-8's lobes, so S = 0. Then L = M + 0 and R = M − 0. Equal levels in both ears = the sound is in the centre.

Move it fully to the left (90°). Why isn't R zero, and why is it negative?

M still hears sound from the side at about half level (0.5), and S hears it fully (+1). R = 0.5 − 1 = −0.5: the right channel gets a quieter, upside-down copy. Your brain still places the sound on the left because L (1.5) is three times louder.

Set the width to 0%. What happens to the virtual microphones?

With no S, L = R = M. Both virtual mics become the same forward-facing cardioid: mono. Raising the width turns them outward. At 50% they point to about ±45°, at 100% to about ±63°.

Tick "Compare with XY". How similar are the decoded MS and XY channels?

Quite similar in the front half, which is why the decoded MS file and the XY file sound alike. MS lets you choose the angle after recording, while XY fixes it when you place the capsules.

Move the sound behind the recorder. Why does it almost disappear?

M faces forward and is nearly deaf at the back, and S is in its blind spot there too. Mid/Side is built to capture what's in front.

Going further

Combining sensors with fixed weights to "aim" a listening direction is called beamforming. Mid/Side is the simplest case, with two sensors and one adjustable weight (the width). Arrays with more capsules (ambisonic microphones, the microphones in a smart speaker) do the same thing with more cups, so they can aim more sharply and in any direction. Getting individual instruments out of a finished mix needs statistics or machine learning, because there the recipes are unknown.

Patterns are idealised: M is a perfect cardioid, S a perfect figure-8 with its + lobe on the left, matching the converter's L = M + S. Real capsules and recorder gain settings vary.
